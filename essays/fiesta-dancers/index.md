A History of the Fiesta Dancers at the University of New Mexico
One important distinction is necessary at the outset: the University of New Mexico’s “Fiesta Dancers” are a public sculpture, not a university dance troupe. The work, officially titled Fiesta Jarabe, depicts a Mexican couple performing a traditional dance and has become a recognizable landmark on UNM’s Albuquerque campus. Its history connects New Mexico’s cultural identity, public art, and debates over artistic representation.
Origins and artistic vision, 1992–1996. 
The sculpture was created by Luis Jiménez (1940–2006), a prominent Mexican American artist born in El Paso, Texas, whose large-scale fiberglass works often explored the people, traditions, and visual culture of the American Southwest. UNM’s Art Museum identifies Fiesta Jarabe as a work produced between 1992 and 1996 and installed on campus in 1996.
The sculpture portrays a man and a woman facing each other in animated dance poses. Their colorful clothing, dramatic gestures, and the sombrero lying between them evoke the jarabe, a traditional Mexican dance commonly associated with the Mexican hat dance. Jiménez used fiberglass, epoxy, and metallic powdered paint to create a vivid, monumental work that could withstand outdoor display while retaining the striking colors characteristic of his art.
Jiménez intended the work to celebrate working-class people of the Southwest. Rather than depicting an abstract or idealized cultural identity, he presented recognizable figures engaged in a popular cultural tradition. The sculpture’s placement beside UNM’s Center for the Arts, which houses Popejoy Hall and the university’s Art Museum, gave it a prominent position at the intersection of campus life and the performing and visual arts.
Public reaction and controversy in 1996
The installation was not universally welcomed. According to a May 10, 1996, article by UNM art history professor David Craven, published in The Albuquerque Tribune, some viewers criticized the sculpture as ugly or inelegant. Craven defended its artistic significance, arguing that such objections reflected narrow expectations about what beauty in public art should look like. The UNM Art Museum’s historical account identifies this contemporary newspaper article as evidence of the debate surrounding the work.
This controversy is an important part of the sculpture’s history. Jiménez’s art challenged conventional representations of the American West by foregrounding Mexican American culture and working-class people. At UNM, Fiesta Jarabe became an example of how public art can provoke disagreement while expanding discussions about whose cultural experiences deserve visibility in shared spaces.
Weathering and restoration, 1996–2019
Over the next two decades, exposure to the elements gradually faded the sculpture’s brilliant colors. According to a University of New Mexico Newsroom report published on February 21, 2019, the work had not undergone restoration since its original installation in 1996. The university therefore commissioned specialists from Silo Workshop, working with the UNM Art Museum, to restore it.
The restoration required careful attention to Jiménez’s distinctive painting technique. Conor Hollis of Silo Workshop explained that the team applied layers of transparent colors to recreate the depth of the original finish. The project renewed the sculpture’s vivid appearance and helped protect it against further deterioration. The university also outlined plans for ongoing maintenance, including supervised cleaning and waxing.
Another notable detail is that Jiménez created five versions of the sculpture. They are not identical: each has small differences, including variations in color. The UNM Archives’ Historic Preservation Committee Photograph Collection documents the campus version as a fiberglass outdoor sculpture dating to 1996.
A lasting campus landmark
Today, Fiesta Jarabe, commonly called the “Fiesta Dancers” or “Fiestas Dancers,” remains part of UNM’s public art collection. Its location beside the Center for the Arts makes it a familiar feature for students, employees, visitors, and audiences attending performances at Popejoy Hall. The sculpture also represents the university’s relationship with New Mexico’s rich artistic traditions and the broader history of Chicano art.
Its story extends beyond its 1996 installation. The original artistic vision, the public controversy documented that year, and the 2019 restoration demonstrate how a work of art can become part of a university’s cultural identity. The Fiesta Dancers continue to invite viewers to consider Mexican American representation, regional identity, and the role of public art in everyday campus life.
